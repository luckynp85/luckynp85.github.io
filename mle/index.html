The likeliest curve

You put ten power supplies on a test bench and write down when each one dies. Now someone asks: "So what's the MTBF?" Behind that simple question hides one of the most beautiful ideas in statistics. Let's find it by playing.

Ten amber dots = ten failure times. The blue curve is a guess at the "true" failure distribution. Some guesses explain the dots well; most don't. Watch the meter.

Here is the whole idea in one sentence, before any math: of all the curves that could have produced your data, pick the one under which your data looks least surprising. That's Maximum Likelihood Estimation. Everything else is just making that sentence precise.

Part one

A curve that "explains" data

Our ten power supplies failed at these times (hours):

410 · 620 · 780 · 890 · 1050 · 1210 · 1480 · 1760 · 2870 · 4520

Electronics like these often fail at random, with a constant hazard rate, so a natural model is the exponential distribution. It has just one knob: the mean life θ (the MTBF). Its probability density is

f(t) = (1/θ) · e−t/θheight of the curve at time t = how "expected" a failure at t is

Now picture each failure as a pin stuck in the time axis. For any guess of θ, look straight up from each pin to the curve. That height measures how plausible that failure is under your guess. A good guess puts the curve high where the pins are.

Top: the model and the data. Bottom: the "likelihood hill", each possible θ scored by how well it explains the data. The shaded band is the 95% likelihood-ratio confidence range. Switch to 40 units and watch the hill get sharper.

Slide θ small and the curve crams all its height near zero; the late failures (2870 h, 4520 h) get tiny stems. Slide it large and the curve flattens; every stem shrinks. Somewhere in between is a sweet spot. For the exponential it lands exactly on the average failure time: θ̂ = 1,559 h. The hat means "our best estimate".

The hill isn't just a way to find the peak. Its shape is your uncertainty. A broad, gentle hill says "lots of θ values are nearly as good." A sharp peak says "the data really pins this down." More data, sharper hill: that's where confidence bounds come from.

Part two

Why everyone takes the log

Why do we multiply the heights? Because the failures are independent, and the probability of several independent things all happening is the product of their probabilities. But look at the readout above: the product is something like 10−33. With a thousand data points it would underflow any computer.

The logarithm fixes this. It turns products into sums, and because log is always increasing, the peak stays at the same θ. So we climb the log-likelihood instead:

ln L(θ) = Σ ln f(ti ; θ)add up the log-heights; find the θ where this sum peaks

That's it. Every MLE you'll ever run (Weibull, lognormal, anything) is this recipe: write down how plausible each data point is, add up the logs, climb to the top.

Part three

The units that didn't fail

Real tests rarely run until everything breaks. Say your manager stopped the test at 1,500 hours. Seven supplies had failed; three were still running. Those three are suspensions (right-censored data). What do you do with them?

Tempting answers: ignore them, or pretend they failed at 1,500 h. Both are wrong, and the likelihood shows why. A suspended unit didn't fail at 1,500 h. It survived past 1,500 h. So its contribution isn't a height on the density curve; it's the probability of surviving that long, the reliability R(1500) = e−1500/θ.

ln L = Σfailures ln f(t) + Σsuspensions ln R(t)failures say "it died here"; suspensions say "it made it at least this far"

Left: each row is one unit. ✕ = failure, ▸ = still running when the test stopped. Right: the likelihood hill for each approach (each scaled to its own peak).

Ignoring survivors throws away the good news and says the supplies are much worse than they are (920 h). Calling them failures is less wrong but still pessimistic (1,094 h). Treating them honestly, as "at least 1,500 h", gives 1,563 h. Hit reveal: when the test was later allowed to finish, the true sample mean was 1,559 h.

This is the superpower of likelihood. It has a natural slot for every kind of partial information: suspensions, interval data ("failed sometime between inspections"), left-censored units. Each gets the right probability term, and the climb does the rest.

Part four

Two knobs: climbing a real mountain

Exponential assumes a constant failure rate. Most mechanical and many electronic things wear out, so reliability engineers reach for the Weibull, which has two knobs: shape β (how the failure rate changes over time) and scale η (the characteristic life, when 63.2% have failed).

With two knobs, the likelihood hill becomes a mountain on a map. Every point on the map below is one (β, η) combination, and brightness is how well it explains our seven failures plus three suspensions. Drag anywhere to try a combination, or let gradient ascent climb for you.

Contour lines are steps of 2 in log-likelihood. The amber diamond is the summit: the MLE. Right: the Weibull density at your point, with failures (✕) and the three suspensions (▸ at 1,500 h).

The summit sits near β ≈ 2.3, η ≈ 1,370 h. β above 1 means the failure rate climbs with age: wear-out. Notice the mountain's ridge is tilted: a slightly bigger β pairs with a slightly different η and fits almost as well. That tilt is the correlation between the two estimates, and it's why joint confidence regions are ellipses, not rectangles.

Software doesn't literally stumble around a map. It solves "slope = 0" with Newton-Raphson or similar. But the picture is exactly right: MLE is the highest point on the likelihood landscape.

Part five

The other way: draw a straight line

Before computers made climbing easy, engineers used Weibull probability paper. Its axes are warped so a Weibull distribution plots as a straight line. Plot each failure at an estimated fraction-failed (the median rank, e.g. Benard's (i − 0.3)/(n + 0.4)), then fit a line. The slope is β.

That's rank regression, a least-squares fit, and it comes in two flavors:

  • RRX: minimize horizontal (time) distances to the line. Recommended by the New Weibull Handbook for small samples.
  • RRY: minimize vertical (probability) distances, the ordinary regression most spreadsheets do.

Pick a dataset and compare them with MLE on the same paper:

Two things jump out. First, with plenty of data every method agrees; the choice matters most when data is scarce or messy. Second, the line methods only "see" the plotted dots. Suspensions nudge the ranks but their survival times never enter the fit, which is why MLE pulls ahead when most of your units are still running.

Part six

So which fit should you use?

MethodIdea in one lineShines whenWatch out
MLEPick the parameters that make the data least surprising.Heavy censoring, interval data, large samples, when you need likelihood-ratio confidence bounds.β is biased high for small samples (fewer than ~20 failures); corrections exist (e.g. RBA).
RRXStraight line on probability paper, errors in time.Small samples, mostly complete data, quick visual check of fit.Ignores survivors' actual times; sensitive to outliers at the ends.
RRYSame, errors in probability.What a spreadsheet trendline gives you; OK as a sanity check.Treats the ranks as the uncertain part, which is backwards for life data.
MomentsMatch the sample mean and variance.Hand calculations, complete data, quick first guess.Can't handle censoring; poor for small samples.

Rules of thumb

  • Always plot the data first. A probability plot shows curvature, mixed failure modes and outliers that no single number will.
  • Small, complete samples: start with RRX; report MLE alongside if you'll need confidence bounds.
  • Lots of suspensions: use MLE. It's the only one here that truly uses how long survivors lasted.
  • Methods disagree a lot? That's information. It usually means too little data, an outlier, or the wrong distribution.

The takeaway: a fit isn't a formula you apply; it's a question you ask. Rank regression asks "which line hugs these dots?" MLE asks "which world makes what I saw most plausible?" For reliability data, full of units that haven't failed yet, the second question is usually the better one.
